01 Homeland & Western Hemisphere+

The Homeland picture is dominated by narco-terrorism and border security. Mexico-based cartels — principally the Sinaloa Cartel and the New Generation Jalisco Cartel — remain the dominant suppliers of fentanyl, heroin, methamphetamine and South American cocaine to the U.S. market, while a wave of independent, semi-autonomous fentanyl producers has fragmented cartel control since 2020. Synthetic opioids killed more than 52,000 Americans in the 12 months to October 2024 — a 33% year-over-year drop that the ATA credits partly to wider naloxone access. China remains the top source of fentanyl precursor chemicals, with India second.

Enforcement has intensified sharply: Southwest border law-enforcement encounters fell 14% in 2024, and Border Patrol apprehensions dropped 85% year-over-year by January 2025. At the same time, cartel violence has escalated in Mexico itself — IED attacks on Mexican security forces surged from just 3 in 2020–2021 to nearly 1,600 in 2024, using landmines, mortars and grenades.

Policy has shifted decisively under the "Trump Corollary to the Monroe Doctrine": treating cartels as designated foreign terrorist organizations, running Operation SOUTHERN SPEAR against narco-terrorists across the hemisphere, and executing Operation ABSOLUTE RESOLVE against Nicolás Maduro's network in Venezuela. The NDS commits to guaranteeing U.S. military and commercial access to the Panama Canal, the Gulf of America, and Greenland, backed by a reshuffled Coast Guard/Navy posture and, where necessary, unilateral lethal action against cartels.

Homeland defense also spans Golden Dome missile and drone defense, nuclear-force modernization, cyber-defense build-out, and a "resource-sustainable" counterterrorism posture aimed only at Islamic terrorist actors with the intent and capability to strike the U.S. directly.

Forward outlook
  • Continued high-enforcement border posture and further cartel-fragmentation violence in Mexico.
  • Additional hemispheric access-assurance actions likely around Greenland, Panama and Venezuela.
  • Golden Dome and counter-UAS development continuing as flagship Homeland-defense programs through the rest of the decade.

Militarily, the PLA fields the DF-27 hypersonic-capable ballistic missile and the PCH191 long-range rocket launcher, and its third carrier, the Fujian, began sea trials in 2024 and is expected to reach full service around 2025–26. In cyberspace, the Volt Typhoon campaign has prepositioned access on U.S. critical infrastructure for use during a future crisis, while Salt Typhoon compromised U.S. telecommunications networks — together, the ATA calls this "the most active and persistent cyber threat" the U.S. faces.

Around Taiwan, Beijing has cut the island's formal diplomatic partners from 22 to 12 since 2016 and continues both economic coercion and military pressure, while confrontations with the Philippines in the South China Sea (2024) and with Japan near the Senkakus keep tensions elevated. China also weaponizes its dominance of critical minerals — banning gallium, germanium and antimony exports to the U.S. in December 2024 in retaliation for chip-export controls — and is racing toward AI leadership (a stated goal of displacing the U.S. as the top AI power by 2030), a $3.3 trillion bioeconomy, and lunar milestones (a 2024 sample-return mission, a crewed landing targeted for 2030, a lunar base targeted for 2035).

The NDS response is deliberately non-confrontational: "deter China in the Indo-Pacific through strength, not confrontation," expanding military-to-military communication with the PLA for stability and de-escalation while building a denial defense along the First Island Chain. The stated goal is not regime change or containment but a sustainable balance of power. The NSS frames this as economically existential too — the Indo-Pacific already generates roughly half of global GDP by purchasing-power parity, and Washington's ambition is to grow the U.S. economy from $30 trillion (2025) to $40 trillion in the 2030s alongside a rebalanced trade relationship with Beijing.

  • Fujian reaching full operational service; PLA's 2027 modernization milestone as a key benchmark to watch.
  • Critical-minerals export restrictions likely to be reused as economic leverage in future frictions.

03 Russia+

Russia views its war in Ukraine as a proxy conflict with the West, and as of the March 2025 ATA had "seized the upper hand," grinding toward continued territorial gains despite catastrophic losses — more than 750,000 dead and wounded since the February 2022 invasion, its worst toll since World War II. Russia's economy nonetheless remains the world's fourth largest by purchasing-power parity, sustained by trade with China, workarounds to sanctions, and continued defense-industrial investment.

Russia retains the world's largest and most diverse nuclear arsenal, a modernized and mobile strategic force, and an expanding non-strategic ("tactical") nuclear stockpile. Its chemical-weapons record includes two documented assassination attempts using Novichok nerve agent — against Sergei and Yulia Skripal in the U.K. (2018) and Aleksey Navalny (2020) — plus hundreds of alleged battlefield chemical-weapons uses against Ukrainian forces since late 2022. Moscow is also developing a satellite-based nuclear antisatellite weapon, first flagged after a February 2022 launch.

Cyber and influence capabilities round out the threat: deep experience integrating cyberattacks with wartime operations, and influence organizations like the Social Design Agency, ANO Dialog and RT working to erode confidence in U.S. elections and institutions. Domestically, Russia's chip industry lags badly (currently ~65nm, targeting 28nm by 2030), pushing deeper technical cooperation with China.

The NDS assesses Russia as a "persistent but manageable" threat to NATO's eastern flank rather than a challenger for European hegemony — non-U.S. NATO's $26 trillion economy dwarfs Russia's $2 trillion — and directs European allies, now committed to the 5%-of-GDP Hague standard, to take primary responsibility for conventional defense and Ukraine support, with the U.S. focused on Homeland defense and China.

04 Iran+

Iran's regional network — the so-called "Axis of Resistance" (Hizballah, Hamas, the Huthis, and Iraqi Shia militias) — has been severely degraded through 2024–25. Israeli strikes on Iranian air defenses in April and October 2024 preceded the collapse of Iran's Syrian ally Bashar al-Assad in December 2024, and by June 2025, Operation MIDNIGHT HAMMER saw U.S. forces strike and, per the NDS, "obliterate" Iran's nuclear program amid Israel's "12-Day War" with Iran. A separate U.S. campaign, Operation ROUGH RIDER, degraded Huthi strike capability enough to compel a ceasefire on Red Sea shipping attacks.

Even so, the ATA (published just before Midnight Hammer) assessed Khamenei had not reauthorized the nuclear-weapons program suspended in 2003, though internal pressure and eroding taboos around the subject were building. The NDS, writing after the strike, states Iran's leaders "have also left open the possibility that they will try again to obtain a nuclear weapon." Iran retains a large stockpile of missiles and UAVs, the region's largest, and continues to threaten shipping through the Strait of Hormuz. Tehran also maintains a long-running vendetta tied to the January 2020 killing of Qods Force commander Qasem Soleimani, and Iranian cyber actors (IRGC) breached a 2024 U.S. presidential campaign-linked email account for influence purposes.

Post-Assad Syria remains volatile: the HTS-led interim government has struggled to unify the country's ethnosectarian groups, and in early March 2025, HTS-aligned and jihadist forces killed more than 1,000 people — mostly Alawite and Christian civilians — in northwestern Syria. ISIS is actively working to exploit that instability to reconstitute.

Strategically, the U.S. now frames the Middle East as a region of opportunity rather than perpetual crisis: Israel as a "model ally" defending itself with limited backing, Gulf states increasingly capable of self-defense, and an expanding Abraham Accords framework.

  • Close monitoring for renewed Iranian nuclear signaling or reconstitution efforts.
  • Proxy-network rebuilding attempts by Hizballah, Huthis and Iraqi militias, even in degraded form.

05 North Korea (DPRK)+

Kim Jong Un's regime is increasingly bound to Moscow. The June 2024 Comprehensive Strategic Partnership treaty includes a mutual-defense clause Kim has cited to justify sending munitions, missiles and thousands of combat troops to fight for Russia in Ukraine — a relationship that also reduces Pyongyang's historic dependence on Beijing and gives North Korean forces rare modern combat experience.

In 2024, North Korea test-fired three intermediate-range ballistic missiles it claimed carried maneuverable hypersonic payloads, part of a steady buildup of its nuclear-warhead stockpile and missile-delivery sophistication. The ATA judges North Korea is "probably prepared to conduct a nuclear test" and continues ICBM flight testing capable of threatening the U.S. Homeland directly. Much of this is funded through large-scale cryptocurrency theft — hundreds of millions of dollars annually — alongside sanctioned trade in labor and UN-proscribed goods.

North Korea's conventional forces remain aging and Soviet-era, but its missile and WMD programs pose a growing, direct threat to South Korea, Japan, and increasingly the U.S. Homeland. Kim has historically relied on non-lethal coercive signaling (missile tests, balloon launches) but retains the option to escalate to lethal asymmetric action, including along the disputed Northern Limit Line.

The NDS assesses South Korea — with its strong economy, defense industry and conscription-based military — as capable of assuming primary responsibility for deterring North Korea, opening the door to a reduced U.S. force posture on the peninsula.

Forward outlook
  • Watch for a possible North Korean nuclear test.
  • Continued missile-testing cadence and deepening technical payoff from the Russia partnership.

06 Transnational Crime & Terrorism+

Even in degraded form since the 2019 defeat of its physical caliphate, ISIS remains "the world's largest Islamic terrorist organization." Its branches vary sharply by capability and momentum: ISIS-Khorasan (South/Central Asia) is the most capable of external attacks and was linked to 2024 mass-casualty attacks in Russia and Iran; ISIS-Somalia doubled in size over the past year; ISIS-West Africa is the largest branch by claimed attacks; and ISIS-Sahel is expanding into coastal West Africa. The group's reach into the Homeland was demonstrated by the January 1, 2025 New Orleans attack and an October 2024 arrest of an Afghan national plotting an Election Day attack.

Al-Qa'ida's network remains active and diversified: AQAP relaunched its "Inspire" propaganda guide, al-Shabaab (its wealthiest affiliate) is deepening ties with the Huthis, and the group is expanding territorial influence in West Africa, including near U.S. personnel in Burkina Faso and Mali.

Financially motivated cybercrime is a parallel and growing threat: a mid-2024 ransomware attack on the largest U.S. healthcare payment processor disrupted prescriptions and even forced ambulance diversions, and both state-linked (2023) and purely criminal (October 2024) actors have struck U.S. water utilities. Transnational criminal organizations also launder billions through digital currencies and engage in human trafficking tied to migrant smuggling debt bondage.

07 Europe & NATO+

The headline shift is burden-sharing: at the June 2025 NATO Hague Summit, allies committed to a new spending standard of 5% of GDP — 3.5% on core military spending plus 1.5% on broader security-related spending. Finland and Sweden's 2023–24 NATO accession extended the alliance's Arctic frontage, and the NDS explicitly wants European allies to take primary responsibility for their own conventional defense, including leading support for Ukraine, with the U.S. providing "critical but more limited" backing while it prioritizes Homeland defense and China.

The NSS frames Europe's challenges as running deeper than underspending, citing a decline in continental Europe's share of global GDP from 25% in 1990 to 14% today, alongside concerns about migration policy, demographic decline, and what it calls a loss of "civilizational self-confidence." Germany's continued reliance on Russian gas processed through Chinese facilities is cited as a cautionary example of the deeper economic entanglements the war has exposed.

On Ukraine specifically, U.S. policy is to press for an "expeditious cessation of hostilities" to stabilize European economies, prevent unintended escalation, and reestablish strategic stability with Moscow — while insisting that responsibility for both the war's resolution and Ukraine's reconstruction sits primarily with Europe.

Forward outlook
  • Continued diplomatic pressure toward a negotiated Ukraine settlement.
  • Allies ramping spending toward the 5% GDP standard through the remainder of the decade.
  • Gradual U.S. force-posture rebalancing away from Europe toward Homeland and Indo-Pacific priorities.

09 Africa+

Africa hosts some of the fastest-growing extremist franchises tracked in the ATA — ISIS-West Africa (the group's largest branch by claimed attacks), an expanding ISIS-Sahel, and al-Qa'ida gaining ground toward urban centers in Burkina Faso and Mali where U.S. personnel are stationed. 10 Technology, Space & Cyber+

This domain cuts across every actor. China's Volt Typhoon and Salt Typhoon campaigns represent the most persistent cyber threat to U.S. infrastructure and telecommunications; Beijing is also pursuing whole-of-government dominance in AI, biotechnology, quantum computing and semiconductors, with a documented history of large-scale intellectual-property theft (as much as 80% of U.S. economic-espionage cases circa 2021 involved PRC entities). Russia, constrained technologically by sanctions, is nonetheless deploying AI-generated deepfakes, AI-enabled counter-drone systems, and battlefield-tested electronic warfare, and is developing a nuclear antisatellite weapon. In space, China has "eclipsed Russia" as the second space power, operating the Beidou navigation constellation, ground-based counterspace weapons (jammers, directed-energy weapons, anti-satellite missiles), and an expanding low-Earth-orbit satellite-internet constellation, while pursuing crewed lunar milestones through 2030 and 2035.

11 The Adversarial Cooperation Axis+

The ATA identifies deepening cooperation among China, Russia, Iran and North Korea as one of the most consequential trends in the threat picture — a set of primarily bilateral relationships that individually reinforce each actor's capabilities while collectively raising the risk that a conflict with any one adversary draws in another. Russia's war in Ukraine has been the accelerant: China provides dual-use materials and components that sustain Russia's defense-industrial base and has grown bilateral trade sharply since 2022; Iran supplies UAVs to Russia in exchange for technical support to its own weapons, intelligence and cyber programs; and North Korea has sent munitions, missiles and thousands of troops to fight for Russia under the June 2024 mutual-defense treaty.

China and Russia's relationship is judged to carry the greatest long-term risk to U.S. interests — more than a decade of combined military signaling has deepened into joint bomber patrols, naval operations, and a November 2024 agreement to expand cooperation on the Arctic's Northern Sea Route, an alternative to Western-dominated shipping lanes. The four also use forums like BRICS and the UN to pursue shared goals such as de-dollarization and to blunt U.S.-led pressure.

The ATA is careful to note this cooperation is uneven — driven mainly by a shared interest in circumventing U.S. power rather than genuine alignment, and tempered by each actor's own concerns about escalation and divergent interests.

Forward outlook
  • Axis cohesion is assessed likely to keep deepening through 2026, independent of the Ukraine war's outcome.
  • Arctic/Northern Sea Route cooperation and BRICS coordination are useful leading indicators to track.

12 U.S. Strategic Posture — The Four Lines of Effort+

The 2026 National Defense Strategy organizes the Department of War's approach around four lines of effort:

1. Defend the U.S. Homeland — secure borders, guarantee access to key hemispheric terrain (Panama Canal, Gulf of America, Greenland), build Golden Dome missile/drone defense, modernize nuclear forces, harden cyber defenses, and maintain focused counterterrorism.

2. Deter China in the Indo-Pacific Through Strength, Not Confrontation — expand PLA military-to-military communication for stability, build a denial defense along the First Island Chain, and enable regional allies, aiming for a sustainable balance of power rather than domination.

3. Increase Burden-Sharing with U.S. Allies and Partners — press NATO toward the 5%-of-GDP Hague standard, push South Korea toward primary responsibility for deterring North Korea, empower Gulf and Israeli partners against Iran, and rely on regional partners to lead against lesser threats in Africa and the Western Hemisphere.

4. Supercharge the U.S. Defense Industrial Base — reshore defense production, expand non-traditional vendors, adopt AI in manufacturing, and leverage allied production capacity to arm both U.S. and partner forces at scale.

Named operations referenced throughout the documents: MIDNIGHT HAMMER (June 2025 strike on Iran's nuclear program), ABSOLUTE RESOLVE (action against Maduro's Venezuela-based network), SOUTHERN SPEAR (Western Hemisphere counter-narco-terrorism), and ROUGH RIDER (campaign that degraded Huthi strike capability and ended attacks on U.S. shipping).
